# Wolfram von Eschenbach (c. 1170–1220) — the humanist

The Middle-High-German poet whose *Parzival* rebuilt the Grail as a **stone** and the cure for
the wounded kingdom as **compassion**, not purity. The most humane version in the canon. *(On
hand: Weston's public-domain verse translation.)*

## The apparatus
- **The suffering Grail-king.** Anfortas, `wolfram-anfortas-wounded` — *"the gentle monarch, who
  was courteous as he was bold"* — reigns in pain over the Grail company; in Wolfram he is wounded
  *for love* (Orgeluse), the desire that maims the keeper of the holy thing.
- **The Grail as a stone, healed by a question of pity.** Where the Vulgate's Grail judges by
  purity, Wolfram's is cured by a human asking, in compassion, what ails the king — the
  *unasked question* reframed from attention (Chrétien) to mercy.
- **Doubt and the road back** (*zwîvel*): the hero's loss and recovery of faith is a human, not a
  doctrinal, arc.

## Steelman
The only Grail that *humans* heal. Against the Vulgate's purge-by-purity, Wolfram makes the holy
reachable through ordinary pity — the canon's strongest argument that transcendence and the human
world need not be at war.

## The standard objection
The Grail-as-stone (*lapsit exillis*) is idiosyncratic and obscure; Wolfram's claimed source
("Kyot the Provençal") is almost certainly invented, so his authority for the most distinctive
Grail is thin.

## How it maps
The great counter-voice on **Cut 3** (the Grail) and **Cut 5** (the wound) — the sharpest contrast
to the Vulgate's punishing Grail. Reads against Chrétien (whose Grail he expands) and against the
Vulgate (whose Grail he humanizes).
