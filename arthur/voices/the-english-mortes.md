# The English Mortes — the Alliterative & Stanzaic *Morte Arthure*

The two fourteenth-century English poems Malory wove into his ending: the **Alliterative Morte
Arthure** (the Roman war and the tragic fall) and the **Stanzaic Le Morte Arthur** (the
love-and-death of the fellowship). Malory's sources for Books V and XVIII–XXI — and the English
elegy for Camelot in their own right.

## The apparatus
- **The Alliterative Morte Arthure** — Arthur as conqueror-emperor brought down: the great Roman
  campaign, the giant of Mont-Saint-Michel, and Arthur's **dream of Fortune's wheel**, which turns
  him from the height to the fall. The martial, tragic Arthur; a chief source of Malory's Roman-war
  books. *(Brock's EETS Middle English; searchable, OCR rough → quote-pending.)*
- **The Stanzaic Le Morte Arthur** — the intimate end: the poisoned apple, the Maid of Astolat, the
  discovery, the war between Lancelot and Gawain, and the partings. Malory's source for the close
  (Books XVIII–XXI), the fall told in lyric stanzas. *(Hemingway's Middle English; searchable, OCR
  rough → quote-pending.)*

## Steelman
These are the English imagination's own reckoning with the fall, and reading them beside Malory
shows exactly what he took and changed (at the end he follows the Stanzaic closely). The
Alliterative Morte is, formally, one of the great English poems of tragic kingship.

## The standard objection
Both survive in difficult copies and reach us as rough Middle-English OCR (Brock 1871; Hemingway
1912) — searchable and locatable, but not yet cleanly quotable at length. Cite via Malory (clean)
and treat the Mortes as the sources *behind* him until cleaned.
