# Sir Thomas Malory (c. 1415–1471) — the keystone / the English spine

The knight-prisoner who compiled the French and English streams into one tragic English book,
*Le Morte d'Arthur*, and registered the **cost** of every motif he inherited. The base's center
of gravity: clean, public-domain, citeable by Book.chapter, and — uniquely — touching **all
seven cuts**. *(On hand: the full Caxton text via Project Gutenberg, Books I–XXI, in two volumes
— `malory1` = I–IX, `malory2` = X–XXI.)*

## The apparatus
- **Two sources of rule, never reconciled — and the loan called back.** The sword in the stone,
  an election by grace — `malory-sword-in-stone`: *"Whoso pulleth out this sword … is rightwise
  king born of all England"* — and Excalibur, an otherworld loan from the Lady of the Lake, which
  at the end is reclaimed: `malory-excalibur-returned`, *"there came an arm and an hand above the
  water and met it, and caught it … and then vanished away the hand with the sword."* Sovereignty
  is half miracle, half debt — and the debt comes due.
- **The fellowship's constitution — and its breach.** The Pentecostal Oath,
  `malory-pentecostal-oath`: *"never to do outrageousity nor murder … but to give mercy unto him
  that asketh mercy."* The book exists to show the best knights break every clause.
- **The Grail that empties the hall.** It enters Camelot in glory — `malory-grail-at-camelot`:
  *"there entered into the hall the Holy Grail covered with white samite, but there was none might
  see it"* — and the quest that follows rides the fellowship out whole and brings it back broken.
- **The wound that lays waste.** The Dolorous Stroke, `malory-dolorous-stroke`: *"three countries
  are destroyed, and … the vengeance will fall on thee"* — one blow sterilizes three kingdoms,
  and is also a debt (`the-cost`).
- **The love defended even as it dooms.** `malory-love-nowadays`: *"love nowadays, soon hot soon
  cold … But the old love was not so; men and women could love together seven years"* — the
  ambivalence intact: the passion is both the court's glory and the lever that cracks it.
- **The prophet who cannot save himself.** Merlin, undone by the enchantment he foresaw —
  `malory-merlin-and-nimue` — the seer of all things destroyed by the one thing he sees; and as
  Merlin goes, the magic goes (`disenchantment`).
- **The return held as doubt.** `malory-rex-quondam`: *"many men say that there is written upon
  his tomb this verse: Hic jacet Arthurus, Rex quondam, Rexque futurus"* — belief reported, never
  asserted. The hope without the claim.

## Steelman
The one voice that touches **all seven cuts** and counts the cost of each — the English canon's
spine, clean and anchor-citeable by Book.chapter. Where the others specialize, Malory holds the
whole tragic arc, founding to fall, in a single register.

## The standard objection
A compiler, not an inventor: the originality is in the *selection and the elegiac tone*, not the
matter, almost all of which he takes from the French (chiefly the Vulgate) and the English
alliterative *Morte*. The book's unity is a translator's achievement, and its seams show.

## How it maps
The crosswalk's **anchor column** for every cut — the keystone that closes the arc the others
open: Geoffrey's frame, Chrétien's eros, Wolfram's compassion, the Vulgate's doom, all resolved
into one English tragedy. Inherits the Vulgate and the alliterative *Morte*.
