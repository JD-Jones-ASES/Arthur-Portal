# Gottfried von Strassburg (c. 1170–1220) — the other adultery

Wolfram's contemporary, whose *Tristan* (Jessie Weston's translation) gives the canon its second
great courtly-love tragedy — the love that is not a choice but a **fate**, drunk from a cup.

## The apparatus
- **The love potion.** Tristan and Iseult love not by will but by magic — `gottfried-love-potion`
  [Tristan (Weston)]: *"a love potion of such power and magic that did any two drink thereof they
  must needs, without will of their own, love each other above all things."* Eros as **compulsion,
  not courtship** — the love that cannot be repented because it was never chosen.
- **The doomed triangle** (Tristan / Iseult / King Mark): the adultery sits at the heart of the
  court, as Lancelot's does at Arthur's — but here the lovers are blameless and damned at once.
  *(Searchable; the potion is the load-bearing quote.)*

## Steelman
The strongest counter-case to the Vulgate's *eros-disqualifies* logic: Gottfried makes the love a
sacrament of its own, sublime and absolute, and dares the reader to condemn what the potion made
inevitable. The paradigm against which Lancelot-and-Guinevere is measured.

## The standard objection
It is, strictly, the Matter of *Cornwall*, not of Britain — Tristan becomes Arthurian only by
absorption (Malory folds in "Sir Tristram," Books VIII–XII, on hand). And the potion can read as an
evasion: does it ennoble the love, or merely excuse it?

## How it maps
Deepens **Cut 4** (the founding betrayal) by doubling it — set Gottfried's *fated* love against
Chrétien's *chosen* worship and the Vulgate's *mutual, Grail-disqualifying* passion. Pairs with
Malory's Tristram (have) and stands beside Wolfram as the German voice.
